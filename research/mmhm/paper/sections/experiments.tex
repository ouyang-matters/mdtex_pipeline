\section{Experimental evaluation}\label{sec:experiments}

The experiments support four claims. The dynamic reduced basis (DRB) is exact
in practice as in theory (\cref{sec:exp-correctness-results}). On workloads
whose edits create and destroy only small cycles, maintaining it is much cheaper
than recomputing the Betti numbers (\cref{sec:exp-performance}). Rebuilding and
the Morse order add further gains under conditions that the ablations identify
(\cref{sec:exp-ablation}). The cost of the representation is not local in
general, as \cref{prop:nonlocal} predicts (\cref{sec:exp-limits}). Detailed
analyses, all result tables and the sensitivity study are in the supplementary
material (numbered S1, S2, \dots); every number, table and figure is regenerated by scripts from the
raw per-update records (\texttt{experiments/README.md}).

\subsection{Setup}\label{sec:exp-setup}

\paragraph{Workloads.}\label{sec:exp-workloads}
Five deterministic generators produce sequences of updates, each a batch of
atomic edits that keeps the complex simplicial (\cref{tab:workloads}). For W1,
W2, W3 and W5 the Betti numbers of every state are known in closed form. W4 is
a flag complex truncated at dimension $D$, so the boundary of every
$(D+2)$-clique is a $D$-cycle that is never filled; $\beta_D/n$ is
\NumWFourBetaDRatioMin{} to \NumWFourBetaDRatioMax{} at the start. W4 therefore
exercises the algebra on non-manifold complexes in which every Betti number
changes; we draw no conclusion about the topology of point clouds from it.
Generator details are in Section~\ref{S-sec:s-setup}.

\paragraph{Methods.}\label{sec:exp-methods}
\Cref{tab:methods} lists the methods; all except \method{gudhi} run in one C++
engine and share its complex store, parser and reduction code. The four DRB
variants separate the mechanisms: \method{drb-id} is the DRB alone, in insertion
order; \method{mmhm-norb} adds the Morse order of $K_0$; \method{drb-id-rb} adds
rebuilds in insertion order; \method{mmhm} has both. Rebuilds follow the policy
of \cref{sec:drb} with $m=\infty$ and $\alpha=1$. The static baselines recompute
all ranks after every update by the reduction with clearing. \method{full-id}
uses the insertion order, which depends on the history of edits and whose cost
drifts upward on W3 and W4; \method{full-sorted} uses a canonical order that
depends only on the current complex. On each instance we take the faster of the
two as the \emph{static reference}. \method{gudhi} computes persistence through
GUDHI's Python interface; it is a reference point for a widely used library, not
the best possible static recomputation, and not our primary evidence. We did not
benchmark implementations of zigzag persistence or of warm-started reductions.

\paragraph{Platform and timing.}\label{sec:exp-platform}\label{sec:exp-timing}
All runs use one cloud virtual machine (Intel Xeon at 2.10\,GHz, 4 cores,
15\,GiB, Ubuntu 24.04, g++~13.3 with \texttt{-O2}), single-threaded, with at most
two timed runs at a time. The engine stores a chain as a sorted array of keys,
so $\Z$ additions are linear merges (\cref{sec:cost}). The time of an update is
the wall-clock time of processing its batch, including any rebuild; parsing,
verification and the initial build are excluded. \method{gudhi} processes only
the first $T'={}$\NumPrefix{} updates on instances above \NumGudhiFullLimit{}
simplices. For each instance and method we pool the per-update latencies of the
three runs and report the \emph{median}, the \emph{p99} and the \emph{amortised
latency}, the total time of all updates divided by their number. A
\emph{speedup} is a ratio of amortised latencies over the same updates; for
\method{gudhi} on a prefix, both methods are taken over that prefix. Slopes are
least-squares fits of $\log$ latency against $\log$ size. A difference is
\emph{within noise} when the per-seed ranges overlap; on W1--W4 the seeds are
different instances, so this is a weak criterion. Repeated identical runs on W2
varied by \NumNoiseCVFullIdAmort--\NumNoiseCVMmhmAmort\,\% (coefficient of
variation of the amortised latency), and by up to \NumNoiseCVAmortMax\,\% on
microsecond-scale updates (Section~\ref{S-sec:s-noise}). Besides time, every run logs
per update the column additions, the footprints of the deletions and
$\nnz(R)+\nnz(V)$; these are proxies for the terms of \cref{prop:cost}.

\paragraph{Correctness checks.}\label{sec:exp-correctness}
Every exact run records its Betti numbers after every update. They are compared
with three references: the engine's \method{full-id} (on every state of every
instance), the closed forms (W1, W2, W3, W5), and GUDHI (all updates up to
\NumGudhiFullLimit{} simplices, the first $T'$ above). The engine also checks
itself against a recomputation every 50 updates, its randomised tests compare
every exact method with a brute-force rank computation, and a pure-Python
implementation of the DRB that shares no code with the engine is cross-checked
on \NumReferenceSamples{} committed samples.

\paragraph{W2: localised updates, constant topology.}
On W2 the Betti numbers are $(1,0,0,0)$ after every update, but each update
consists of $16s$ atomic edits, and every edit changes one Betti number
(\cref{thm:exact}(d)). The DRB latency depends only weakly on the size of the
complex: over five sizes the median grows with slope
\NumSlopeEOneWTwoSEightMmhmMed{} to \NumSlopeEOneWTwoSEightDrbIdMed{}, against
\NumSlopeEOneWTwoSEightFullSortedMed{} and \NumSlopeEOneWTwoSEightFullIdMed{}
for the static baselines (\cref{fig:scaling}(a)). The speedup over the static
reference therefore grows with the complex, from
\NumMainWTwoSmallestSpeedupMin--\NumMainWTwoSmallestSpeedupMax{} at
\NumWTwoNSevenSEightSize{} simplices to
\NumMainWTwoLargestSpeedupMin--\NumMainWTwoLargestSpeedupMax{} at
\NumWTwoNThirtyThreeSEightSize. All four DRB variants are within noise of each
other here, and no rebuild fired: the gain is that of the dynamic basis itself,
not of the Morse order or of rebuilds. The DRB cost grows linearly with the edit
size (slopes \NumSlopeETwoWTwoNTwentyTwoMmhmMed{} to
\NumSlopeETwoWTwoNTwentyTwoDrbIdRbMed{} in $s$; \cref{fig:scaling}(b)), whereas
recomputation hardly depends on it; at $s=64$, that is $1024$ atomic edits per
update, the DRB variants are still
\NumWTwoNTwentyTwoSSixtyFourMmhmSpeedupFullId--\NumWTwoNTwentyTwoSSixtyFourDrbIdRbSpeedupFullId{}
times faster than \method{full-id}; larger batches were not tested. Against
\method{gudhi} on the same prefix, \method{mmhm} is
\NumWTwoNThirtyThreeSEightMmhmSpeedupGudhi{} times faster at the largest size, a
ratio that also reflects GUDHI's richer output and its interface. Because the
topology of W2 does not change from update to update, W2 alone does not show the
behaviour under topology changes; W3 and W4 do.

\paragraph{W3: changing topology and a growing basis.}
On W3 every carving creates a cavity and every refill destroys one. Here the
maintained basis grows: without rebuilds, $\nnz(R)+\nnz(V)$ rises over a run at
$N=20$ from \NumWThreeNTwentyDrbIdNnzStart{} to \NumWThreeNTwentyDrbIdNnzEnd{}
(\method{drb-id}), and the latency rises with it (\cref{fig:mechanism}(b)).
Rebuilds triggered by this growth (\NumWThreeNTwentyMmhmRebuilds{} per run for
\method{mmhm}) reset the basis and keep the latency from drifting upward.
\method{mmhm} is the fastest method at every W3 size,
\NumMainWThreeMmhmSpeedupMin{} to \NumMainWThreeMmhmSpeedupMax{} times faster
than the static reference, which is \method{full-sorted} here; at $N=27$ it is
\NumWThreeNTwentySevenMmhmSpeedupFullSortedFirstBlock{} and
\NumWThreeNTwentySevenMmhmSpeedupFullSortedLastBlock{} times faster over the
first and the last 200 updates, and its p99 is lower as well (\cref{tab:summary}).
The variants without rebuilds are slower than \method{full-sorted} at the
smallest size and have a higher p99 there; \cref{sec:exp-ablation} separates the
contributions of rebuilding and of the order.

\paragraph{W4: non-manifold complexes in which every Betti number changes.}
The DRB variants are faster than the static reference on every W4 instance,
\NumMainWFourSmallSpeedupMin{} to \NumMainWFourSmallSpeedupMax{} times at
$n=1000$ and \NumMainWFourLargeSpeedupMin{} to \NumMainWFourLargeSpeedupMax{}
times at $n\ge4000$, and their median latency hardly depends on the size
(slopes \NumSlopeEFourWFourPlaneDTwoMmhmNorbMed{} to
\NumSlopeEFourWFourPlaneDTwoDrbIdRbMed{} against
\NumSlopeEFourWFourPlaneDTwoFullIdMed{} for \method{full-id}, three sizes;
\cref{fig:scaling}(d)). Their p99 is below that of the static reference except
on the two instances with $n=1000$ and $D=3$, where the difference is within
noise. No rebuild fired under the default policy, and on the larger instances
the Morse order makes the DRB faster (\cref{sec:exp-ablation}).

\paragraph{The dynamic basis.}
Tracking the change of basis is what makes incremental maintenance possible,
and on W2 and W4 it carries most of the gain: in insertion order and without
rebuilds, \method{drb-id} is \NumWTwoNThirtyThreeSEightDrbIdSpeedupFullSorted{}
times faster than the static reference on the largest W2 instance and
\NumWFourPlaneNSixteenThousandDTwoDrbIdSpeedupFullId{} times on the largest W4
instance (where the best variant reaches
\NumWFourPlaneNSixteenThousandDTwoMmhmNorbSpeedupFullId).

\paragraph{Rebuilding.}
Rebuilding pays off where the basis grows. On W3 it is the largest single
effect: at $N=20$, switching the growth trigger on ($\alpha=1$) takes the
insertion-order DRB from \NumESevenWThreeNTwentyDrbIdRbAOffPOffAmort{} to
\NumESevenWThreeNTwentyDrbIdRbAOnePOffAmort\,\textmu s and the Morse-ordered one
from \NumESevenWThreeNTwentyMmhmAOffPOffAmort{} to
\NumESevenWThreeNTwentyMmhmAOnePOffAmort\,\textmu s amortised. Where the basis
does not grow (W2, W4) the trigger does not fire, and forced periodic rebuilds
only add cost: every 64 updates, they raise the amortised latency of
\method{mmhm} on W2 from \NumESevenWTwoNTwentyTwoSEightMmhmAOnePOffAmort{} to
\NumESevenWTwoNTwentyTwoSEightMmhmAOnePSixtyFourAmort\,\textmu s. On the
surfaces of W1, rebuilds help on the smaller instances but not on the largest
(Table~\ref{S-tab:rebuild-effect}).

\paragraph{The Morse order.}
\Cref{lem:apparent} and its corollaries say where the work of a build is done;
they do not make it smaller than in a good insertion order. Our generators write
structured insertion orders, and at build time the Morse order saves almost
nothing against them: on W2 it avoids the
\NumWTwoNThirtyThreeSEightDrbIdBuildAdds{} additions of the insertion order
(\NumWTwoNThirtyThreeSEightMmhmNorbBuildAdds{} instead), on W1, W3 and W5 both
orders need the same number of additions within one percent, and on W4 the Morse
order needs \NumWFourPlaneNOneThousandDThreeBuildAddsMorseOverId{} to
\NumWFourSpaceNFourThousandDThreeBuildAddsMorseOverId{} times as many
(Table~\ref{S-tab:build}). What matters at build time is that the order is
structured: in a random order
(\method{drb-rand}) the basis has
\NumWFourPlaneNFourThousandDTwoNnzInitRandOverId{} to
\NumWThreeNFourteenNnzInitRandOverId{} times as many entries after the build,
and updates on W1--W3 are \NumWThreeNFourteenDrbRandOverDrbIdAmort{} to
\NumWTwoNFifteenSEightDrbRandOverDrbIdAmort{} times slower than in insertion
order (Table~\ref{S-tab:rand}). Between rebuilds the Morse order helps where the
basis grows or carries large cycles: \method{mmhm-norb} is faster than
\method{drb-id} on W3 with $N\ge20$, on W4 in the square with $n\ge4000$ and on
the large W1 surfaces, within noise on W2, and slower on the W5 witnesses
(\cref{tab:ablation}). At a rebuild it helps on W3, where \method{mmhm} is
faster than \method{drb-id-rb} in \NumESevenWThreeNTwentyMmhmBelowDrbIdRb{} of
the \NumESevenWThreeNTwentySettings{} settings of the policy sweep. The
combination of growth-triggered rebuilds and the Morse order, \method{mmhm}, is
the fastest variant on W3 at every size; on W4, where nothing triggers a
rebuild, \method{mmhm-norb} is. There \method{mmhm}, which did not rebuild
either, is slower than \method{mmhm-norb} beyond noise; we have not profiled
this difference (Section~\ref{S-sec:e4}).

\subsubsection{Where does locality break down?}\label{sec:exp-limits}

\paragraph{Non-locality witnesses.}
On W5 every toggle creates or destroys a cycle that spans the complex, and
\cref{prop:nonlocal} forces every DRB state to do work linear in its size. The
measurements follow it: the amortised latency per toggle grows with slope
\NumSlopeESixWFiveCycleDrbIdAmort{} to \NumSlopeESixWFiveCycleDrbIdRbAmort{} on
the cycle ($L=10^3$ to $10^6$) and \NumSlopeESixWFiveSphereDrbIdRbAmort{} to
\NumSlopeESixWFiveSphereMmhmAmort{} on the sphere (\cref{fig:nonlocality}), for
every order and rebuild policy. Recomputation is linear as well, so the DRB
remains faster on average (\cref{tab:summary}), but its p99 is higher on the
cycle (at $L=10^6$, \NumWFiveCycleLOneMillionMmhmPNinetyNine{} against
\NumWFiveCycleLOneMillionFullIdPNinetyNine\,\textmu s for \method{mmhm}) and, for
the Morse-ordered variants, on the largest sphere
(\NumWFiveSphereLEightMmhmPNinetyNineOverFullId{} times that of
\method{full-id} for \method{mmhm}). The bound concerns this representation,
which stores cycle representatives explicitly; it is not a lower bound for
dynamic Betti numbers. On a graph, for instance, $\beta_0$ and $\beta_1$ follow
from dynamic connectivity in polylogarithmic amortised
time~\cite{holm2001polylog}.

\paragraph{Surfaces.}
W1 shows a related effect on a workload with local edits. Opening the first
hole in a closed surface destroys a $2$-cycle that spans the surface
(\cref{prop:nonlocal}), and the median latency of every DRB variant grows with
the complex almost like that of recomputation (slopes
\NumSlopeEFiveWOneSphereMmhmNorbMed{} to \NumSlopeEFiveWOneSphereDrbIdRbMed{}
against \NumSlopeEFiveWOneSphereFullIdMed{} on the sphere); we did not log the
chain sizes that would explain this. The amortised speedup over the static
reference ranges from \NumMainWOneSpeedupMin{} to \NumMainWOneSpeedupMax, and
every DRB variant has a higher p99 than the static reference on every W1
instance, by factors \NumMainWOneProbNinetyNineRatioMin{} to
\NumMainWOneProbNinetyNineRatioMax. For connected surfaces with known
connectivity, combinatorial counters give the Betti numbers in $O(1)$ per edit
without any matrix (\cref{rem:surfaces}). The full W1 results are in
Section~\ref{S-sec:s-surfaces}.
