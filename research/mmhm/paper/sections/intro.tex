\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

The Betti numbers $\beta_0,\beta_1,\beta_2,\beta_3$ of a simplicial complex count
its connected components, independent loops, enclosed voids and, in dimension
three, independent $3$-cycles; over a field they are determined by the ranks of
the boundary matrices~\cite{munkres1984elements,hatcher2002algebraic,edelsbrunner2010computational}.
In many uses the complex is not fixed. Adaptive meshes are refined and
coarsened by local moves~\cite{pachner1991pl}, cavities are carved into a volume
mesh and refilled, holes are opened in a surface and closed again, and the
proximity complex of a point cloud changes as the points
move~\cite{carlsson2009topology}. After each modification one wants to know
whether it changed the topology: whether it created a void, closed a tunnel or
disconnected a component. Recomputing the Betti numbers from scratch answers
this, but it reads the whole complex after every modification, however small
the modification is.

Over a field, $\beta_k=n_k-r_k-r_{k+1}$, where $n_k$ is the number of
$k$-simplices and $r_k$ the rank of the $k$-th boundary matrix~$\Bm{k}$.
Maintaining Betti numbers therefore means maintaining ranks, and the problem has
more structure than general dynamic rank: the boundary of a simplex does not
change while the simplex exists, so each atomic edit inserts or deletes one
column of one boundary matrix, and rows appear and disappear only while they are
zero (\cref{obs:structural}). Insertions alone are handled by reducing the new
column against the existing pivots, as in the standard persistence
algorithm~\cite{edelsbrunner2002topological,zomorodian2005computing}: each
inserted simplex either creates a cycle or destroys
one~\cite{delfinado1995incremental}. Deletions are the difficulty. After reduction, a column is a sum of original
columns; when one of these is deleted, every reduced column that absorbed it
becomes invalid, and the reduced matrix alone does not record which columns
these are.

\paragraph{Version 1 and this version.}
Version~1 of this paper~\cite{ouyang2025mmhm_v1} reduced the complex once to the
critical cells of a discrete Morse matching~\cite{forman1998morse,forman2002user},
re-reduced after each edit only the critical columns directly affected by it,
recompressed periodically, and bypassed linear algebra with combinatorial gates
where Betti numbers follow from counts. It stated that this yields exact Betti
numbers at a cost governed by the size of the edit. The update rule is not
exact, for two independent reasons. It re-reduces a replaced column but not the
reduced columns that had absorbed it, which is the deletion problem described
above; and its set of affected critical columns misses columns whose Morse
boundary changes through gradient paths that end far from the edit. Its
experiments could not reveal either defect, because in each of them the Betti
numbers were constant or followed from the Euler characteristic.
\Cref{sec:erratum} states what version~1 specified and gives concrete
counterexamples to both exactness claims. This version replaces the method, its
analysis and its experiments. It keeps the title because discrete Morse theory
still has a role, though a different one.

\paragraph{Approach.}
For each dimension $k$ we store a reduced matrix $R=\Bm{k}V$ together with the
invertible change of basis~$V$, and for every simplex $\sigma$ the set
$\occ(\sigma)$ of columns of $V$ that contain it, the \emph{footprint}
of~$\sigma$ (\cref{fig:method}). An insertion reduces the new column. A deletion
rewrites every column in the footprint of the deleted simplex: if one of them is
a cycle, that cycle is added to the others (Case~A); otherwise the column with
the smallest pivot is added to the others (Case~B). This keeps the Betti numbers
exact after every atomic edit, for every order of the cells. The order affects
only the cost, and this is where discrete Morse theory enters. When the
structure is built or rebuilt, we compute an acyclic matching by collapses and
order the cells by reverse removal time. Under this order every gradient pair is
an apparent pair, so the static reduction performs all its column additions in
the columns of critical cells, and on these columns, after following the
gradient flow, it performs exactly the additions of the reduction of the Morse
complex. The Morse complex itself is never formed; its boundary is what an edit
can change far away from the edit.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),leftmargin=2.2em]
\item An exact \emph{dynamic reduced basis} (DRB) for the $\Z$ Betti numbers of
  a simplicial complex under insertions and deletions of simplices, implemented
  for dimension at most three, with complete proofs
  (\cref{thm:exact,cor:rebuild}; the arguments hold in every dimension).
  Insertion is the standard incremental reduction. The deletion step is the
  rank-only specialisation of the deletion updates used for zigzag persistence
  and for warm-started $R=DV$
  factorisations~\cite{carlsson2009zigzag,luo2023warmstart,giunti2026pruning};
  we prove it in a self-contained way because the basis tracking it relies on is
  what version~1 omitted.
\item The Morse order as a sparsifier. Ordering the cells by reverse removal
  time of a collapse makes every gradient pair an apparent pair
  (\cref{lem:apparent}); hence, at a rebuild, all column additions take place in
  columns of critical cells (\cref{cor:critical-work}), and there they coincide
  with the reduction of the Morse boundary matrices
  (\cref{cor:morse-reduction}). Between rebuilds the order is a heuristic, and we
  measure its effect.
\item Output-sensitive costs instead of a locality theorem: the cost of an edit
  in terms of footprints and reduction chains (\cref{prop:cost}), and explicit
  families, a cycle graph and a triangulated sphere with one toggled simplex, on
  which every update of size one costs this data structure $\Omega(n)$
  operations (\cref{prop:nonlocal}).
\item An evaluation on five workload families (punctured surfaces; refinement,
  coarsening and cavities in a $3$-ball; flag complexes of moving points;
  non-locality witnesses) against static recomputation, including
  GUDHI~\cite{maria2014gudhi}, and against ablations of the order and of the
  rebuild policy. Every exact method is checked against three independent
  oracles (\cref{sec:experiments}).
\item An erratum to version~1 with concrete counterexamples to its update rule
  for each of its two defects, and a reimplementation of the rule whose error
  rate we measure (\cref{sec:erratum,sec:e0}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{What we do not claim.}
All statements are over $\Z$; we say nothing about integer homology or torsion.
We prove no bound on the cost of an update in terms of its size, and
\cref{prop:nonlocal} shows that none holds for this data structure. The
maintained basis yields ranks and a basis of each cycle space, but no
persistence pairing. Between rebuilds we make no sparsity claim.

\paragraph{Outline.}
\Cref{sec:related} reviews related work. \Cref{sec:theory} defines the dynamic
reduced basis and proves its exactness, introduces the Morse order, analyses the
cost of an update, proves that no locality bound holds, and ends with the
erratum to version~1 (\cref{sec:erratum}). \Cref{sec:experiments} describes the
experimental setup and results, \cref{sec:discussion} discusses limitations and
alternatives, and \cref{sec:conclusion} concludes.
