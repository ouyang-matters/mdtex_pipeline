\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

We have replaced the localised update of version~1, which is not exact, by a
dynamic reduced basis that maintains the $\Z$ Betti numbers of a simplicial
complex exactly under insertions and deletions of simplices. Insertion is the
standard reduction step; deletion corrects every basis chain that contains the
deleted simplex, which is the rank-only form of known zigzag and warm-start
updates. Exactness holds for every order of the cells, and the cost of an update
cannot be bounded by its size. Discrete Morse theory enters only through the
order chosen at a build or rebuild, where it locates the column additions in
critical columns.

The experiments support the exactness and qualify everything else. Every Betti
vector reported by an exact method was compared with at least one reference,
\NumCoverageExternalPct\,\% with one outside our engine, and none disagreed. An
ablation that applies version~1's re-reduction rule without basis tracking
errs within the first \NumLcrFirstWrongMaxAll{} updates on surfaces, 3-balls and
flag complexes, and with version~1's periodic recompression (the periodic trigger only,
without its locality trigger or gates) it is still wrong
after most updates on surfaces, cavities and flag complexes. Where the edits
touch only small cycles, the dynamic reduced basis is fast and nearly independent of the size of the complex: on the largest
W2 instance all its variants are
\NumWTwoNThirtyThreeSEightDrbSpeedupFullIdMin--\NumWTwoNThirtyThreeSEightDrbSpeedupFullIdMax{}
times faster than static recomputation, so this gain owes nothing to the Morse
order or to rebuilds. Where the cost grows with the complex, on surfaces and on
the non-locality witnesses, the average gain is at most a constant factor or
negative and the tail latency is often worse; on surfaces with known
connectivity, combinatorial counters are the better tool. The Morse order saved
nothing at build time against the insertion orders of our generators; between
rebuilds it helped on surfaces, cavities and the larger flag complexes and hurt
on the witnesses. Whether the order is structured at all mattered more on
surfaces, refinement and cavities: built in a random order, the basis made
updates up to
\NumWTwoNFifteenSEightDrbRandOverDrbIdAmort{} times slower. On the cavities the
gain over static recomputation is smaller against a canonical order, which does
not drift with the history, than against the insertion order. Rebuilding paid
off on cavities, did not trigger on refinement and flag complexes, and on
surfaces helped the smaller instances but not the largest.

The results are limited to $\Z$ coefficients, to complexes of dimension at most
three, to one machine and to single-threaded runs; no locality bound holds, the
logged costs are proxies, and the rebuild policy is a heuristic. Open questions
are maintaining the matching between rebuilds, so that the guarantees of
\cref{lem:apparent} hold at all times; rebuild policies with provable
guarantees; bounds on footprints and reduction chains for structured families of
complexes and edits; other coefficients; and a comparison with implementations
of zigzag persistence.
