\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Two regimes.}
On W2, W4 and the larger W3 instances, the latency of the DRB methods depends
little on the size of the complex. On W5, \cref{prop:nonlocal} forces a cost
linear in the size of the toggled cycle, and the measurements follow it. On W1
the median cost of an update also grows with the complex, although typical
updates create only small cycles; we have not measured why (E5). Where the cost
grows with the complex, recomputation is also linear, so the DRB keeps at most a
constant factor: on W1 at most \NumWOneSphereLFiveMmhmSpeedupFullId, with several
variants slower than recomputation, and on W1 and W5a its tail latency is worse.
Five workload families do not settle which properties of a workload decide the
regime.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Coefficients and dimension.}
Every result of this paper is over $\Z$. Version~1 discussed integer
coefficients, torsion and the Smith normal form, and described its update as
working over a chosen coefficient ring; nothing in it supports these statements,
and we withdraw them. Ranks over a field do not see torsion, which requires the
Smith normal form over the integers~\cite{munkres1984elements}, and Betti
numbers over $\Z$ can differ from those over other fields: the real projective
plane has Betti numbers $(1,1,1)$ over $\Z$ and $(1,0,0)$ over the
rationals~\cite{hatcher2002algebraic}. We have neither implemented nor tested
other coefficients. The proofs of \cref{sec:theory} hold in every dimension, but
the engine and all experiments are restricted to dimension at most three.

\paragraph{No locality theorem, and proxies for cost.}
By \cref{prop:nonlocal} the cost of an update is bounded only in
output-sensitive terms (\cref{prop:cost}), and what we log are proxies for those
terms: counts of additions and footprints, not the sizes of the chains involved
(\cref{sec:cost}). The proxies and the time can disagree (E7). Where no spanning
cycle is created the proxies stayed small: on the W2 and W4 instances of E8 the
median number of column additions per update was at most
\NumEEightWFourPlaneNSixteenThousandDTwoDrbIdAddsMed. Nothing guarantees this for
other workloads.

\paragraph{Rebuild policy and the Morse order.}
The growth threshold $\alpha$ and the period $m$ are heuristics whose effect
depends on the workload (E7). A method that may rebuild in the Morse order also pays, we
believe, for the index such a rebuild needs (E4). \Cref{lem:apparent} and its
corollaries locate the work of a build in the critical columns; they do not make
it smaller than in a good insertion order, and on our workloads it was not
(E8). Between rebuilds the Morse order is a heuristic whose effect ranges from
clearly positive (large surfaces, W3, larger W4) to negative (W5); for one-shot
static recomputation the collapse never paid for itself (E1).

\method{gudhi} runs through its Python interface

\paragraph{Measurements.}
All timings come from one virtual machine, single-threaded runs and one compiler
setting. Run-to-run variation is a few percent on most instances we repeated and
much larger on microsecond-scale updates (\cref{sec:exp-timing}); we mark
differences within the per-seed ranges as within noise. One run was an outlier,
which a rerun did not reproduce (E1). The counters do not depend on the machine.
We did not measure memory; as a proxy, $\nnz(R)+\nnz(V)$ reached at most
\NumWTwoNThirtyThreeSEightMmhmNnzPerSimplex{} entries per simplex on the largest
W2 instance, \NumWOneTorusFiveHundredDrbIdNnzPerSimplex{} on the largest torus
(\method{drb-id}) and \NumWThreeNTwentySevenDrbIdNnzPerSimplex{} on the largest
W3 instance (\method{drb-id}), against \NumWThreeNTwentySevenMmhmNnzPerSimplex{}
for \method{mmhm} there. W4 is truncated at dimension $D$, so it measures the
algebra on non-manifold complexes, not the topology of point clouds
(\cref{tab:w4-betti}).

\subsection{When to use what}\label{sec:when}

\paragraph{Surfaces with known connectivity:} combinatorial invariants
(\cref{rem:surfaces}). On the largest W1 sphere, \method{euler} was
\NumWOneSphereLSevenEulerSpeedupFullId{} times faster than static recomputation
and faster still than every DRB method.

\paragraph{Barcodes:} zigzag persistence~\cite{carlsson2010zigzag}.
$\beta_k(K_t)$ is the number of $k$-dimensional intervals of the zigzag barcode
that contain~$t$, so zigzag
algorithms~\cite{carlsson2009zigzag,milosavljevic2011zigzag,maria2024discrete,dey2022fast}
answer our query as well, the incremental algorithm of Carlsson, de Silva and
Morozov~\cite{carlsson2009zigzag} after every update. We did not compare
against zigzag implementations.

\paragraph{Connectivity only:} fully dynamic connectivity, which maintains
$\beta_0$ (and, for graphs, $\beta_1=n_1-n_0+\beta_0$) in polylogarithmic
amortised time per edge edit~\cite{holm2001polylog}; on W5a no method that
stores cycle representatives can match this. For complexes in the $3$-sphere
built by insertions only, the algorithm of Delfinado and
Edelsbrunner~\cite{delfinado1995incremental} needs near-linear total time.

\paragraph{Small complexes, or tail latency under global cycles:} static
recomputation with clearing (\method{full-id}), not in the Morse order. It stores
no basis, it was faster on average than \method{drb-id} on the smallest W3
instance, and it had the lower p99 on every W1 instance and, beyond noise in all
but one case, on every W5a instance. Within the
edit sizes we measured ($s\le64$ on W2) it did not overtake the DRB methods on
average; we did not test larger batches.

\paragraph{Edit-local updates on large general complexes:} a dynamic reduced
basis. On W2 every variant did equally well, so the gain there comes from the
basis itself, not from the Morse order or rebuilds. Where the basis grows, as on W3,
the growth-triggered rebuild in the Morse order (\method{mmhm}) was the fastest
method at every size; on the larger W4 instances and the large W1 surfaces the
Morse order without rebuilds (\method{mmhm-norb}) was at least as fast; on the
non-locality witnesses the insertion order was faster. The DRB handles
non-manifold complexes in which every Betti number changes, and on W2 and W4 its
latency hardly depended on the size of the complex.
