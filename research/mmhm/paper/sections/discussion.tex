\section{Discussion and limitations}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Where the method is effective.}
The dynamic reduced basis replaces recomputation by work on the chains that an
edit touches. When those chains stay small, as on refinement, carving and the
flag complexes, the latency of an update depends little on the size of the
complex and the gain over recomputation grows with it. When an edit creates or
destroys a cycle that spans the complex, the work is linear in that cycle
(\cref{prop:nonlocal}), recomputation is linear too, and only a constant factor
remains, with a heavier tail. Our five workload families mark these two regimes
but do not settle which properties of a workload place it in one or the other.

\paragraph{Basis sparsity and rebuilding.}
The cost of an update is governed by the size of the maintained basis, and on
workloads whose edits change the topology the basis can fill in over time.
Rebuilding resets it; a growth-triggered policy fired only where fill-in
occurred and paid off there, whereas rebuilds on a basis that does not grow are
pure overhead. Even where rebuilding pays off, rebuilding too often raises the
tail latency above that of recomputation (\cref{fig:rebuild-policy}). The
threshold and period are heuristics, and no setting was best on every workload.

\paragraph{The role of the Morse order.}
The order of the cells does not affect correctness (\cref{thm:exact}), and
\cref{sec:morse} shows what the Morse order guarantees at a build: every
gradient pair is apparent and the work concentrates in the critical columns.
Empirically, a random order made updates much slower on surfaces and
refinement, more than the choice between two structured orders; on cavities and
flag complexes the two effects were of similar size. The Morse order improved on
insertion order between rebuilds and at rebuilds where the basis grows or
carries large cycles, and not elsewhere. We
make no sparsity claim for the state between rebuilds, and maintaining the
matching under edits, which would extend the guarantees of \cref{sec:morse} to
all times, remains open.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
All results are over $\Z$: ranks over a field do not see torsion, and Betti
numbers over $\Z$ can differ from those over other fields (the real projective
plane has $(1,1,1)$ over $\Z$ and $(1,0,0)$ over the
rationals~\cite{hatcher2002algebraic}). The proofs hold in every dimension, but
the engine and the experiments are restricted to dimension at most three. The
cost of an update is bounded only in output-sensitive terms, and what we log are
proxies for those terms. All timings come from one virtual machine, with
single-threaded runs and one compiler setting, and memory was not measured.
The baselines are static: we compared with recomputation and with GUDHI, but not
with implementations of zigzag persistence or warm-started factorisations, which
also support insertions and deletions and compute richer
output~\cite{carlsson2009zigzag,luo2023warmstart}. Our measurements therefore
show an advantage over recomputation, not over these dynamic algorithms.

\paragraph{Alternatives.}
Specialised structures are preferable where their assumptions hold: fully
dynamic connectivity for $\beta_0$, and for $\beta_1$ of
graphs~\cite{holm2001polylog}; the incremental algorithm of Delfinado and
Edelsbrunner for insertion-only complexes in the
$3$-sphere~\cite{delfinado1995incremental}; combinatorial counters for
connected surfaces of known connectivity (\cref{rem:surfaces}); and zigzag
persistence when the barcode of the whole sequence is
needed~\cite{carlsson2010zigzag}. For small complexes, or when the tail latency
matters on workloads with spanning cycles, static recomputation with clearing
remains competitive. The dynamic reduced basis is suited to edit-local updates
of large general complexes whose Betti numbers are needed online.
